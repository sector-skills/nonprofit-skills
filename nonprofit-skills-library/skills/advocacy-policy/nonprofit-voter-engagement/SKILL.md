# Nonprofit Voter Engagement

## When to Use This Skill

Use this skill for any nonprofit activity that touches elections or voting, always through a
strict nonpartisan lens:

- Planning and running a voter registration drive (in-person, online, or via program participants)
- Building nonpartisan voter education materials: side-by-side candidate questionnaires, ballot
  measure explainers, "where to vote" / "how to register" guides
- Hosting a candidate forum or debate where all qualified candidates are invited on equal terms
- Designing a GOTV (get-out-the-vote) reminder campaign: registration deadline alerts, early-voting
  reminders, polling place information
- Training staff, board, or volunteers on the 501(c)(3) bright lines around election activity before
  an election cycle
- Reviewing planned content (social posts, newsletters, event descriptions) for partisan-neutrality
  risk before it publishes

**Boundary — hand off when the ask is:**
- Advocacy on a specific bill or regulation (not tied to a candidate election) → `nonprofit-legislative-advocacy`
  for direct engagement or `nonprofit-policy-analysis` for the underlying research
- A general (non-election) action alert or petition campaign → `nonprofit-grassroots-mobilization`
- Structuring a 501(c)(4) sister entity specifically to engage in activity a (c)(3) legally cannot
  (e.g., candidate endorsement) → `nonprofit-c3-c4-structure`

## The Core Compliance Rule

Under IRC 501(c)(3), an organization is **absolutely prohibited** — with no expenditure test, no
election, no exception — from participating or intervening in any political campaign on behalf of or
in opposition to any candidate for public office. This is a categorically different (and stricter)
rule than the 501(h) lobbying expenditure limits that apply to legislative advocacy: there is no
dollar threshold here — a single clearly partisan act can jeopardize exempt status. Every activity
under this skill must be designed against that absolute bar, not a "limit."

## The Nonpartisanship Test — Apply to Every Activity

Before running any voter engagement activity, check it against these IRS-recognized factors (drawn
from the facts-and-circumstances test the IRS actually uses):

1. **Even-handedness across candidates/parties** — invite or cover all qualified candidates/parties
   equally; never limit an activity to one candidate or party
2. **No statements of approval/disapproval of any candidate** — before, during, or in connection with
   the activity, from staff acting in their organizational capacity
3. **Timing and scope not tied to one candidate or election** — a registration drive run in the same
   pattern every cycle (not suddenly ramped up only in a district with one favored candidate) reads
   as genuinely nonpartisan
4. **Broad, not targeted, audience** — registration/GOTV efforts should target underregistered
   populations generally (by geography, age, community served) rather than lists correlated with
   predicted support for one candidate/party
5. **Neutral, factual content only** — voter guides must present candidates' own statements/records
   side by side without editorializing, rating, or scoring in a way that implies endorsement

Any single activity can pass or fail this test on its own facts — there is no safe harbor from
volume or good intentions; document the even-handedness of each activity (invitation lists, materials
used) in case of audit.

## Standard Activity Designs

1. **Voter registration drive:**
   - Identify target population by non-partisan criteria (unregistered clients/community members
     generally, new adult program participants, etc.) — never a list segmented by likely political
     support
   - Use the nonpartisan National Voter Registration Form or state equivalent only; never a
     party-branded registration form
   - Train volunteers with a scripted, neutral pitch: register-to-vote only, no discussion of
     candidates or issues at the registration table
   - Track only registration-activity metrics (forms distributed/collected), not who registered with
     which party — collecting that data creates its own risk of appearing to build a targeting list

2. **Candidate forum:**
   - Invite every legally qualified candidate for the race in writing, with equal advance notice
   - Use a neutral, pre-announced, identical question set for every candidate (submitted by the
     audience/moderator, not the org's staff editorializing)
   - If a candidate declines or doesn't attend, note their empty podium/seat neutrally rather than
     filling their time with commentary
   - Neutral moderator (not a staff member who has made public statements about the race)

3. **Voter guide / ballot measure explainer:**
   - Present each candidate's or side's own words/positions verbatim or via direct citation, not the
     org's paraphrase or scoring
   - For ballot measures (not candidates), the org MAY take a position — 501(c)(3)s can support/oppose
     ballot measures as this is issue advocacy, not candidate campaign intervention, though it still
     counts toward 501(h) lobbying limits if applicable (coordinate with `nonprofit-legislative-advocacy`)
   - Clear disclaimer: "This guide does not endorse any candidate" printed on every version

4. **GOTV reminders:**
   - Registration deadline, early-voting dates, and polling-place-lookup tool links — factual and
     non-candidate-specific
   - Distribute through the org's normal channels at the same relative volume as prior non-election
     communications (a sudden GOTV-only surge aimed at one demographic can raise targeting concerns)

## Common Failure Modes

- **Staff personal political activity bleeding into org channels.** A staff member's personal
  endorsement is fine on their own time/account but must never use org letterhead, logo, email
  signature, or official social account — train this distinction explicitly before every cycle.
- **"Issue scorecards" that function as de facto endorsements.** Rating candidates on org-selected
  issues and publishing a ranked or graded scorecard is a frequent, high-risk drift from neutral
  voter guides into de facto candidate opposition/support.
- **Selectively inviting candidates.** Forums or questionnaires sent to only the incumbent or only
  one party's candidates are a clear violation even if unintentional (e.g., "we couldn't reach the
  other campaign in time" — document the equal-effort attempt).
- **Targeting registration drives by predicted partisanship.** Using voter file data to target
  registration/GOTV efforts at likely-supporter demographics rather than broadly underregistered
  populations undermines the nonpartisanship defense even if messaging stays neutral.
- **Confusing ballot-measure advocacy with candidate restrictions.** Boards sometimes wrongly assume
  the same absolute bar applies to ballot measures; it doesn't — measures are issue advocacy (subject
  to 501(h) lobbying limits, not the categorical candidate ban), while candidates are an absolute bar.
- **No pre-cycle staff training.** Election-cycle violations most often come from a well-meaning but
  untrained staffer's offhand social post, not a deliberate governance decision — refresh training
  every cycle, not just once.

## Practitioner vs. Advisor Application

- **As the practitioner (ED, program, or comms staff):** Run every planned election-cycle activity
  through the five-factor nonpartisanship test before launch, document even-handedness (invite lists,
  identical scripts/questions), and hold a refresher training for all staff/board every election
  cycle, not just new hires.
- **As an advisor/consultant:** When a client wants to "do more" during an election cycle, first
  separate what's an absolute bar (candidate intervention) from what's merely capped (issue/ballot
  measure lobbying under 501(h)) — many clients self-censor legitimate ballot-measure advocacy out of
  confusion with the candidate rule, while others under-appreciate that there's no safe dollar amount
  for candidate intervention. Recommend a documented pre-clearance review step for any content
  mentioning a candidate by name during an active election cycle.

## Example

**Input:** "We want to host a mayoral candidate forum and also do a voter registration push at our
community center this fall."

**Output approach:** Confirm all legally qualified mayoral candidates are invited in writing with
equal notice; build one neutral question set submitted by attendees; recruit a moderator with no
public statements on the race; design the registration drive to target all unregistered community
center visitors generally (not filtered by any predicted political lean), using the standard
nonpartisan registration form and a scripted neutral volunteer pitch; add the "does not endorse any
candidate" disclaimer to all forum and registration materials; schedule a 30-minute staff/volunteer
training on the nonpartisanship rules before the drive launches.
