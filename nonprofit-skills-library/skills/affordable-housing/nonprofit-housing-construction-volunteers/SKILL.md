# Volunteer Home Construction Program (the Habitat Build Model)

## When to Use This Skill

Use when the user says, in their own words:

- "plan our build season across three sites without half the volunteers standing around"
- "draft a run-of-show for Saturday's build day"
- "how do I turn my Saturday regulars into crew leaders?"
- "what can unskilled volunteers safely do, and what do we reserve for licensed trades?"
- "write the tailgate safety talk for new volunteers"
- "how should we deploy our AmeriCorps members?"
- "the partner family starts on their own build Saturday — how do we run that?"
- "who's bringing lunch, and do we have enough water and restrooms for 40 people?"

Covers the volunteer-powered new-construction cycle and produces four standard deliverables: build-season calendar, build-day run-of-show, site safety orientation outline, and crew-leader training outline.

**Boundary.** This skill covers volunteer construction operations only. Route out by exact name: general volunteer recruitment, retention, recognition, screening, and volunteer-program infrastructure → `nonprofit-volunteer-management`; ReStore and other retail volunteer staffing → `nonprofit-retail-staffing`; paid contractor scopes, critical home repair, weatherization, and preservation programs → `nonprofit-housing-repair-preservation`; site control, entitlements, pro formas, and the capital stack → `nonprofit-housing-development-finance`; sweat-equity policy design (hour requirements, qualifying activities, alternatives to construction hours) → `nonprofit-housing-homeownership-programs`.

## The Operating Model

Five roles appear on every well-run volunteer build site; name them in any plan so accountability is explicit:

1. **Construction supervisor** (paid staff or lead contractor) — sole quality authority; sets the task list, signs off scopes, decides what volunteers may touch.
2. **Volunteer coordinator** (paid staff or AmeriCorps) — owns calendar, sign-ups, waivers, food, and communication.
3. **Crew leaders** (experienced volunteers or AmeriCorps members) — one task each with 4–8 volunteers; teach, correct, and enforce safety.
4. **Skilled-trade leads** — licensed or deeply experienced volunteers/subcontractors who own electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and other technical scopes.
5. **Partner families** — future homeowners working construction hours as crew members, never as a substitute for volunteers.

Plan throughput honestly: a task a skilled worker finishes in an hour can absorb a five-person volunteer crew for a full morning once orientation, breaks, correction, and rework are included. Over-plan parallel tasks so no crew idles waiting for materials or an inspection.

## Build-Season Scheduling Across Multiple Sites

The build season is scheduled backward from inspection gates, not forward from volunteer enthusiasm. Typical sequence per house, with volunteer-friendliness noted:

1. Foundation, slab, or crawl space — usually paid subcontractors. Low volunteer value.
2. Framing (walls, roof deck) — classic volunteer phase; high output, visible progress.
3. Rough-in MEP (electrical, plumbing, HVAC) — licensed trades; volunteers only assist (drilling, staging) where the trade lead and local code allow.
4. Insulation — volunteer-friendly. Rough-in inspection must pass first.
5. Drywall hang and finish — the heaviest volunteer phase (hanging is volunteer work; taping/finishing coats often paid for quality).
6. Interior finish: paint, trim, doors, cabinets, flooring, fixtures — peak volunteer phase.
7. Landscaping, fencing, punch list, final cleanup — volunteer-friendly, all ages with supervision.

**Stagger the sites.** On any given peak volunteer day (usually Saturday), each active site should be in a volunteer-friendly phase. Never schedule a big volunteer day when every site is in a licensed-trade or inspection-hold phase.

**Steps — build a build-season calendar:**

1. List each house with its permit-issued date and target closing date. Done when every house has a dated spine from foundation to final.
2. With the construction supervisor, map inspection gates (footing/foundation, framing, rough-in MEP, insulation, final) onto each spine. Done when each house shows its inspection dates and 3–5 buffer days after each gate for rework.
3. Mark volunteer-friendly windows per house. Done when every Saturday from opening to close shows at least one site in a volunteer-friendly phase.
4. Set volunteer capacity per site-day: one crew leader per 4–8 volunteers, and only as many crews as the supervisor can support. Done when each site-day has a named crew count, not a raw headcount.
5. Fix the no-volunteer days: paid-trade phases, inspection holds, and weather-fallback zones (freeze dates, monsoon or rain seasons, extreme-heat windows). Done when the calendar shows them explicitly so recruitment doesn't book against them.
6. Publish the calendar 6+ months out with named phases, not just dates, so corporate sponsors and faith groups book against tasks they want. Done when the sign-up system shows phase names per day.

Failure mode: a calendar built on house-completion math alone. Volunteer crews do not accelerate licensed-trade or inspection phases — a day where 30 volunteers show up during rough-in with no assist tasks planned produces idle volunteers who do not return.

## Task Design for Unskilled Volunteers

Two tests decide every task: **tolerance** (how forgiving the work is of imperfect execution) and **consequence of error** (what a mistake costs in safety, code compliance, or rework). Assign volunteers tasks high on both scales: forgiving and cheap to fix.

**Volunteer-friendly (standard practice):** staging materials; insulation; drywall hanging and screwing; painting and caulking; siding (under crew leader); decking and fencing; trim, doors, and shelving; base cabinets and countertop installation with supervision; window installation under an experienced crew leader; landscaping, mulch, sod, and cleanup; punch-list painting.

**Reserve for paid or licensed trades, always:** foundation and concrete work; structural connections, headers, truss setting, and any engineered system; electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and gas scopes (the license holder of record pulls the permit and owns the work — a licensed volunteer may serve, but the scope stays under the licensed contractor); drywall finish coats where visible quality drives warranty complaints; roofing where pitch, height, or weather raises fall risk beyond volunteer capacity; anything the local code official restricts to licensed trades.

**Steps — design a volunteer task card:**

1. Define the scope in units a crew can finish in one day (e.g., "hang drywall on walls of house 2, second floor" not "drywall"). Done when each crew has a bounded, countable scope.
2. List tools, materials, and quantities with delivery staging time. Done when the supervisor confirms materials are on site two days ahead.
3. Write the teaching sequence (demonstrate, guided practice, then work). Done when a crew leader can run the task from the card alone.
4. Name the quality check and who signs off. Done when the supervisor's end-of-day walk is on the card.
5. Name the safety requirements (PPE, height limits, tool restrictions). Done when the card cross-references the site safety orientation.

## Crew-Leader Recruitment and Development

Crew leaders are the program's scarcest resource; a site can absorb more unskilled volunteers than it can absorb first-time crew leaders. Build a visible ladder: one-day volunteer → repeat volunteer → assistant crew leader → crew leader → house leader. People step up when the next rung is named and offered.

**Recruit from:** retired tradespeople and site foremen; skilled members of faith-community and corporate sponsor groups; home-builders' association and trade-association members; and your best repeat volunteers regardless of trade background. Select for teaching temperament and calm under correction as much as construction skill — a crew leader's real job is instruction and safety enforcement, not personal productivity.

**Steps — develop crew leaders:**

1. Track repeat volunteers with a simple skills-and-interests note at sign-in. Done when every repeat volunteer has a development suggestion in the record.
2. Invite prospects to serve as assistant crew leaders under an experienced house leader for 2–3 build days. Done when each prospect has completed at least two shadow days.
3. Run the crew-leader training (outline below) before their first solo crew. Done when the training attendance and a signed safety-commitment form are on file.
4. Assign solo crews on low-risk tasks first (painting, insulation, landscaping), then structural (framing, decking), then finish. Done when each crew leader's progression is logged.
5. Debrief after every solo day: what the crew finished, injuries or near misses, volunteer coaching needs. Done when the volunteer coordinator has logged the debrief within 48 hours.

**Crew-leader training outline (deliverable):**
1. The role: teacher and safety officer, not fastest hammer. Quality standards and when to call the supervisor.
2. Running the task card: demonstration, guided practice, work assignment, end-of-day quality check.
3. Safety enforcement: fall protection at 6 feet, ladder and scaffold rules, tool orientations and sign-offs, PPE, minor-volunteer rules, stopping unsafe work without embarrassment.
4. Incident response: injury protocol, near-miss reporting, first-aid kit location, emergency numbers.
5. People management: mixed-skill crews, partner families in the crew, volunteers who won't take direction, hydration and rest breaks.
6. Handshake with the supervisor: morning scope confirmation, mid-day check, end-of-day walk and sign-off.

## Skilled-Trade Volunteer Recruitment

Licensed scopes do not become volunteer scopes because a licensed person is present — the licensed volunteer (or paid sub) is the contractor of record for the permit. Recruit accordingly:

1. Ask trade and builders' associations, union locals, and apprenticeship programs for "trade-lead days" — the licensed pro leads, volunteers assist within code and trade-lead comfort. Done when each licensed scope has a named trade lead a month out.
2. Ask suppliers and big-box pro desks for demonstrations and trade referrals. Done when each build phase's material supplier has been asked once.
3. Publish "assistant under licensed lead" as the offer — many tradespeople volunteer precisely to teach. Done when skilled-volunteer sign-ups list trade and license status.
4. Confirm with the local building official what assisting is permitted on your permit before the day. Done when the trade lead confirms the plan in writing.

## Jobsite Safety for Volunteers

Unpaid volunteers are generally not OSHA-covered employees, and OSHA will not cite a site for volunteer safety — but adopt OSHA construction standards as affiliate policy anyway: your insurer, your quality standards, and any paid staff on site pull you into them, and a volunteer injury is indistinguishable from a worker injury in consequence. State-plan states may add stricter rules.

**Non-negotiables (standard affiliate practice, aligned to OSHA):**

- **Fall protection at 6 feet.** 29 CFR 1926.501(b)(13) requires conventional fall protection — guardrails, safety nets, or personal fall arrest — for residential construction work 6 feet or more above a lower level. Apply it to volunteers as policy: open floor edges, window and stair openings, and roof work get guardrails, hole covers, or harness systems before crews go up.
- **Ladders and scaffolds.** Follow OSHA Subpart X (ladders) and Subpart L (scaffolds): inspect before use, maintain three points of contact, extend ladder rails 3 feet above landings, never stand on the top two rungs.
- **Tool orientations.** No one uses a power tool without an orientation and sign-off from the crew leader for that tool; orientation includes what goes wrong (kickback, binding, blade guards) not just how to start it.
- **PPE.** Safety glasses when cutting or nailing, hearing protection with saws, task-appropriate gloves, hard hats when overhead work is active, closed-toe boots always.
- **Minor volunteers.** Standard affiliate practice: no one under 16 on an active construction site; volunteers 16–17 may not use power tools, work above 6 feet, or do roofing; minors need a parent/guardian-signed waiver and defined adult supervision ratios. Set and publish your written policy; enforce age checks at sign-in.
- **Site discipline.** First-aid kit and a trained first responder on every site day; posted emergency numbers and site address; no children or pets on active sites; no volunteers in trenches or on ladders without assigned purpose.

**Site safety orientation outline (deliverable) — run it every build day, every crew, no exceptions:**
1. Welcome and day's scope (2 min) — what the house will look like at 4 p.m.
2. Site hazards now (3 min) — today's specific: open edges, overhead work, saw zones, weather.
3. PPE and tools (5 min) — issue PPE, power-tool sign-up, "no orientation, no tool."
4. Safety rules (5 min) — 6-foot rule, ladder use, keep work area clean, minor rules, no roof work without a crew leader's go.
5. If someone gets hurt (2 min) — stop work, call the supervisor, first-aid kit location, incident report.
6. Logistics (3 min) — water, breaks, restrooms, lunch time, end-of-day cleanup.
7. Questions, waiver/age verification, and split to crews.

**Incident response steps:**
1. Stop work in the affected area; render first aid; call 911 for anything beyond minor. Done when the injured person is treated and the area is secured.
2. Notify the construction supervisor and affiliate director same-hour. Done when both acknowledge.
3. Record on the incident report within 24 hours: names, witnesses, photos, task, tool, and safety controls present. Done when the report and near-miss log entries are filed.
4. Notify the insurer per policy timelines. Done when the claim number or a documented no-claim decision exists.
5. Debrief within one week: root cause, control fix, and what changes in the safety orientation. Done when the orientation outline is updated.

## AmeriCorps Member Deployment

Habitat's AmeriCorps construction crew leader program places full-time members for roughly a 10–11 month service year (typically starting August–September), serving Tuesday–Saturday on build sites, leading skilled and unskilled volunteers and homeowner families. Members earn a living allowance and the Segal Education Award; positions are contingent on annual grant funding — confirm your affiliate's current-year grant before building the calendar around member labor.

**Deploy members as:** daily site leads when the supervisor must split across multiple sites; crew-leader trainers (members multiply your crew-leader pipeline); volunteer coordinators who own the run-of-show on peak days; and quality-control runners between sites.

**Manage them correctly:** AmeriCorps members are not employees — no at-will discipline, no employee benefits, hour and duty limits set by the member agreement. Performance problems go through the grant's member-support and grievance process with your state commission, not your HR manual. Members may not displace staff; a member quietly doing the construction supervisor's job puts the grant out of compliance.

## Partner-Family Participation on Their Own Build

Partner families work construction hours on their own home and on others'; scheduling them is a logistics task, while hour requirements and qualifying activities are policy (route those to `nonprofit-housing-homeownership-programs`).

**Steps — run the family's first build day:**
1. Book the family into regular crews weeks in advance, around work shifts and childcare, with the same sign-in and waiver as any volunteer. Done when their dates appear on the build calendar as named crew members.
2. Place them with your best crew leader, not your newest. Done when the assignment is explicit on the run-of-show.
3. Offer meaningful scope on their own home — framing their walls, hanging their drywall — plus a moment (photo, wall-signing) that the family chooses. Done when the day's task card names the family's tasks.
4. Coach crew leaders in advance on tone: the family are future homeowners and co-workers, not supervised beneficiaries. Done when the crew leader debrief confirms the framing you set.
5. Track hours against the family's sweat-equity plan the same day, under the family record rather than an anonymous sign-in. Done when hours appear in the log by end of day.

## Food and Hospitality Logistics

Hospitality is the program's retention engine; volunteers return for the work and the welcome.

1. Water: one station per 15–20 volunteers, checked hourly on hot days; shade or indoor rest area; electrolytes above 85°F. Done when the run-of-show names a hydration owner.
2. Lunch: confirm provider, headcount, delivery time, and serving plan in writing 48 hours out; keep donated food simple (sandwiches, pizza) to limit food-safety risk; label dietary options. Done when the confirmation is attached to the run-of-show.
3. Restrooms and handwashing: portable units serviced for peak headcount; handwash stations near food and before interior finish work. Done when service dates cover every build day.
4. Registration and welcome: sign-in table with waivers, name tags, PPE issue, and a greeter who knows the day's plan. Done when a first-time volunteer is oriented within 15 minutes of arrival.
5. End-of-day thanks: 5-minute wrap — what got done, what the house needs next, next sign-up ask. Done when the wrap is on the run-of-show.

## Coordinating with the Construction Supervisor and Inspection Schedule

The supervisor owns the scope; the volunteer coordinator owns the people; the run-of-show is the weekly contract between them.

**Weekly handshake:**
1. 10–14 days out, the supervisor issues the task list per site-day (scope, crews, materials, gates). Done when every upcoming Saturday has named task cards.
2. The coordinator recruits to the task cards and confirms crew leaders against them. Done when each crew has a leader 7 days out.
3. Confirm the inspection calendar weekly; never schedule a volunteer scope behind an inspection that hasn't passed. Done when each gated task has its inspection status marked.
4. Build a fallback task bank per site (cleanup, staging, landscaping, paint prep, de-nailing lumber) for weather, failed inspections, and no-show crews. Done when each site-day lists two fallback tasks that need no materials delivery.
5. Day before: confirm materials on site, tools staged, food, water, restrooms. Done when a pre-day checklist has come back complete.

**Build-day run-of-show (deliverable):**

| Time | Item | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| 6:45 | Supervisor + crew leaders walk scope; materials and safety check | Construction supervisor |
| 7:15 | Registration, waivers, PPE issue, coffee | Volunteer coordinator |
| 7:45 | Safety orientation (full outline above) | Supervisor or house leader |
| 8:00 | Crews split to tasks; tool orientations per crew | Crew leaders |
| 9:45 | Mid-morning break; supervisor rounds all crews | Supervisor |
| 12:00 | Lunch; family/sponsor moment; next-steps announcement | Coordinator |
| 12:30 | Afternoon work; supervisor passes failed-scope decisions | Crew leaders |
| 2:30 | Hydration check on hot days | Coordinator |
| 3:30 | Crew close-out; quality walk; tools returned and counted | Supervisor + crew leaders |
| 4:00 | Site sweep, trash-out, tool and material lockdown, wrap talk | Supervisor |

## Common Failure Modes

- **Idle volunteer day during a paid-trade phase.** Remedy: stagger sites by phase; keep a fallback task bank; cut recruitment before you cancel it.
- **Crew leader with 12 volunteers.** Remedy: hard cap at 8, split the crew or move volunteers to landscaping/cleanup, and track ratios on the run-of-show.
- **Roof work with untrained crews, wet lumber, or wind.** Remedy: reserve roofing for experienced house leaders with fall protection rigged; wind and rain cancels it without debate.
- **Minors with power tools or on ladders.** Remedy: enforce the written age policy at sign-in with a visible chart; crew leaders stop it on sight.
- **Safety orientation skipped because the crew is eager.** Remedy: it is on the run-of-show with a time and an owner; volunteers don't start until it has run.
- **Volunteers scheduled behind a failed inspection.** Remedy: mark inspection status on every task card; fallback tasks absorb the day.
- **A licensed scope quietly shifted to volunteers to save budget.** Remedy: the permit holder of record runs the scope; the supervisor, not the budget, decides task assignment.
- **AmeriCorps member disciplined like staff or used as a staff substitute.** Remedy: route performance issues through the state-commission member process; audit duties quarterly against the member agreement.
- **Partner-family hours bottlenecked in the last month before closing.** Remedy: book family crew dates at season start and track monthly against their sweat-equity plan.
- **First-time drywall or trim crew's rework exceeds the labor saved.** Remedy: pair a finish-quality crew leader, demo on a scrap surface first, and route finish coats to paid labor where warranty quality demands it.

## Practitioner vs. Advisor

Practitioners (affiliate construction and volunteer staff): use the steps as daily operating discipline; the run-of-show, task cards, and handshake cadence are the working artifacts. Advisors and consultants assessing an affiliate: ask for one Saturday's run-of-show, the written youth and fall-protection policies, the incident and near-miss log, and the crew-leader roster with shadow-day counts — those four artifacts reveal more about program health than any mission statement.
