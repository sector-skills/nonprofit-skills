# Faith-Based Nonprofit Pastoral Communications

## When to Use This Skill

Use this skill when the user is drafting, or helping clergy draft, a message that is expected to
land in a **faith voice** rather than a brand or executive voice. The audience is a congregation,
a parish, a synagogue or mosque community, a denominational membership, a faith-based
organization's staff and constituents who share (or partly share) the tradition, or the family and
friends of someone the community is mourning or celebrating. Trigger phrases include: "draft a
pastoral letter," "note to the congregation about [Name]'s death," "how do we announce the
funeral," "should we say anything about the shooting / election / verdict," "Ramadan greeting to
staff," "Rosh Hashanah message to donors," "Holy Week email," "letter from the bishop," "farewell
letter from Pastor X," "welcome letter for the new rabbi," "the imam wants to write to families
this week," "the ED needs a word after last night's tragedy," "letter to the congregation about
the investigation," or "prayer chain wording for a member in hospice."

Boundary: this skill is the **pastoral voice**. Secular executive communications from the ED seat
— all-staff memos, funder updates, town-hall talking points, layoff announcements, board-facing
statements — are `nonprofit-executive-communications`, even for a faith-based org, whenever the
message is in the org's executive register rather than a shepherd register. Brand voice, taglines,
and outward-facing marketing copy are `nonprofit-brand-messaging`. Press releases and reporter
relationships during a clergy-misconduct crisis are `nonprofit-media-relations`; this skill
handles the pastoral letter to the congregation about the same event, coordinated with but distinct
from the press piece. Giving letters, tithe appeals, pledge campaigns, and annual contribution
statements are `nonprofit-faith-stewardship-giving`. The governance-side work of drafting,
debating, voting, and adopting a denominational statement — committees, floor process, canonical
approvals — is `nonprofit-faith-church-governance`; this skill takes the adopted decision and
writes the pastoral letter that carries it. Mandatory-reporter rules and clergy-penitent privilege
mechanics live in `nonprofit-faith-employment-ministerial-exception`; touch them here only to
route the user out to that skill.

## Core Frameworks

Name the frame before drafting. A pastoral message written against the wrong frame reads as tone
deaf even when every sentence is technically correct.

- **Pastoral voice vs. brand vs. executive vs. media voice.** Four distinct registers a
  faith-based org uses depending on who is speaking and to whom.
  - **Pastoral voice**: first-person singular ("I write to you today…"), shepherd stance,
    presence over performance, meaning-making rather than positioning, lament permitted, care for
    the reader over control of the narrative. Signed by clergy or by an ED explicitly stepping
    into a pastoral role. Silence and brevity often more pastoral than volume.
  - **Brand voice**: third-person org identity ("[Org] has always stood for…"), controlled by
    marketing/communications, aimed at differentiation, uses tagline language, avoids first-person
    grief. Wrong register for a death or a tragedy.
  - **Executive voice**: first-person plural ("Our team learned yesterday…"), signed by the ED,
    optimized for staff/board/funder clarity, names decisions and next steps. Right for a layoff
    or a strategic pivot; wrong for a memorial.
  - **Media voice**: third-person, quotable, on-the-record, drafted with counsel, aimed at
    reporters rather than the community. A pastoral letter is not a press release; when both are
    needed for the same event, draft them separately and coordinate the facts.
- **The "should we say anything" pastoral decision test.** Five questions in order: (1) Does the
  community expect a word from clergy on this? (2) Does our tradition typically speak on matters
  of this kind? (3) Is silence itself a message in this moment — silence about a local shooting
  reads as absence, silence about a distant partisan controversy may read as restraint? (4) Who
  is uniquely served by hearing from us that no other voice serves? (5) What will we regret
  saying, or not saying, in six months? If four of five point toward speaking, speak. If two or
  fewer, hold.
- **Prophetic speech vs. partisan endorsement.** Faith traditions have long traditions of
  naming public wrongs (poverty, violence, injustice) from within the tradition's own moral
  vocabulary — this is prophetic speech and is squarely inside 501(c)(3) territory. Endorsing or
  opposing a candidate for public office, coordinating with a campaign, or rating candidates on a
  scorecard tied to an election crosses the **Johnson Amendment** (IRC §501(c)(3) absolute
  prohibition on political-campaign intervention) and can jeopardize exempt status. When drafting
  named-issue statements near an election, route the user to a lawyer or to
  `nonprofit-faith-church-governance` for the political-activity discipline before publishing.
- **Christian liturgical calendar (Western)**: Advent (four Sundays before Christmas) → Christmas
  → Epiphany (Jan 6) → Ordinary Time → Ash Wednesday → Lent (40 days) → Holy Week (Palm Sunday,
  Maundy Thursday, Good Friday, Holy Saturday) → Easter → Eastertide (50 days) → Pentecost →
  Ordinary Time. Eastern Orthodox calendars shift Pascha and use the Julian calendar in many
  jurisdictions; do not conflate dates.
- **Jewish calendar (lunisolar; dates shift each secular year)**: weekly Shabbat (Friday sunset to
  Saturday sunset); High Holy Days (Rosh Hashanah, Ten Days of Awe, Yom Kippur) in the fall;
  Sukkot; Shemini Atzeret / Simchat Torah; Hanukkah (winter); Tu B'Shevat; Purim; Passover (Pesach,
  spring, eight days in Diaspora); Counting of the Omer; Shavuot; Tisha B'Av (summer fast). Reform,
  Conservative, Orthodox, and Reconstructionist communities observe with different expectations
  around work, driving, and electronic communication — a Yom Kippur email that lands Friday
  evening will not be read until Sunday in many communities.
- **Islamic calendar (Hijri, lunar; dates shift ~11 days earlier each Gregorian year)**: Muharram
  (including Ashura); Rabi' al-Awwal (Mawlid, observed in some communities and not others);
  Ramadan (month of fasting) → Eid al-Fitr; Dhu al-Hijjah (Hajj) → Eid al-Adha. Ramadan and both
  Eids are the two moments a US faith-based org that serves or employs Muslims most needs a
  pastoral or greeting word; check moonsighting sources (or the community's convention) before
  fixing a date.
- **Denominational vs. congregational locus of authority.** A message from the local clergy speaks
  for the congregation; a message from a bishop, presbytery, conference minister, denominational
  executive, or head of communion speaks for the wider body. On some matters (a local death) only
  the local voice can speak; on others (a policy of the tradition) only the denominational voice
  can bind; on many the two must be aligned in advance to avoid mixed signals.
- **Clergy-penitent privilege and mandatory reporting.** Privilege is state-by-state and is
  narrowed or abolished for child-abuse disclosures in many states; do not draft any pastoral
  message that references confidential disclosures without routing the user to
  `nonprofit-faith-employment-ministerial-exception` for the reporting analysis first.

## Standard Deliverables

Every request in scope resolves into one of these artifacts. Pick the artifact deliberately —
using the wrong artifact for the moment is the most common failure.

- **Funeral / memorial / death notice** — clergy to congregation and family; ED to staff on the
  death of a colleague, board member, or major stakeholder.
- **Wedding, baptism, bar/bat mitzvah, aqiqah, or life-milestone announcement** — in the
  tradition's typical framing, with consent from the family.
- **Illness or hospitalization notice** — with strict privacy discipline; only what the person or
  family has authorized.
- **Crisis and tragedy response** — local violence, mass-casualty events, natural disasters,
  national moments (elections, mass shootings, hate crimes, verdicts).
- **"Should we say anything" pastoral decision memo** — the short internal note that resolves
  whether and how clergy speaks on a given moment.
- **Clergy misconduct disclosure to the congregation** — coordinated with legal, insurance, and
  denominational counsel; drafted alongside (never in place of) the press response.
- **Denominational statement / bishop's letter / pastoral letter** — from a bishop, denominational
  executive, or head of communion on a theological, moral, or policy matter.
- **Farewell letter (outgoing clergy) and arrival letter (incoming clergy)** — the pastoral
  bookends of a clergy transition.
- **Sermon-adjacent weekly congregation email** — the note from the pulpit that extends the
  Sabbath/Sunday/Friday message into the week.
- **Prayer request / prayer-chain content** — with confidentiality discipline about what may and
  may not be shared, and by whom.
- **Liturgical-season greeting or letter** — Advent, Lenten, Easter, High Holy Days, Ramadan,
  Eid, or the org's chosen cadence pegged to its constituents' traditions.
- **Building or institutional milestone** — dedication, cornerstone laying, mortgage-burning,
  deconsecration, closure of a congregation, merger with another congregation.

## The "Should We Say Anything" Pastoral Decision Checklist

Run this before drafting when the trigger event is a public moment (a shooting, a hurricane, an
election result, a verdict, a denominational controversy, a national tragedy). Do not run it for
routine pastoral events (a member's death, a wedding, a scheduled holy day) — those already
require a word.

1. **Name the event in one sentence, and name the community that will read the message.** "A
   mass shooting at a synagogue 2,000 miles away; our congregation is a Reform synagogue in a
   city with an active Jewish federation." Specificity here prevents drafting a generic message
   that lands nowhere.
2. **Answer the five questions.** (a) Does this community expect a word from clergy on this? (b)
   Does our tradition typically speak on matters of this kind? (c) Is silence itself a message
   here? (d) Who is uniquely served by hearing from us? (e) What will we regret saying, or not
   saying, in six months? Write one honest sentence for each. If four of five point toward
   speaking, speak; if two or fewer, hold; if three, escalate to the clergy for a judgment call.
3. **Distinguish prophetic speech from partisan endorsement.** If the moment is inside 60 days of
   a federal election and the message would name a candidate, party, or scorecard, stop and route
   the user to counsel and to `nonprofit-faith-church-governance` for Johnson Amendment analysis
   before continuing.
4. **Pick the channel.** Pulpit-only, pulpit-plus-follow-up-email, email-only, bulletin note,
   short website statement, or silence. Default hierarchy for a pastoral moment: the pulpit
   carries the word; a short plain email follows for those who weren't there; social media
   usually stays quiet or reposts the email. Reverse only for reasons.
5. **Set length.** Two to three short paragraphs is right for most public-moment pastoral letters;
   five paragraphs is almost always too many. If the clergy wants more, ask what a member reading
   this on a phone at 6:45 a.m. needs — and cut the rest.
6. **Draft, then let the clergy own it.** Draft in the clergy's honest voice (see "Voice fidelity
   when a staffer drafts" below), not in HR or comms boilerplate. The clergy is the author of
   record and must be able to read the message aloud without wincing.

## Drafting a Congregational Death Notice — Numbered Checklist

The single most consequential pastoral communication a clergy person writes. Run this every time.

1. **Confirm family consent and preferred wording before writing anything public.** Ask the family
   what they want said, what may be shared about circumstances (especially in a suicide, overdose,
   or violent death), how the deceased is to be named (given name, Hebrew name, kunya, honorifics),
   and whether they want the announcement to precede or follow their own notifications to extended
   family. Nothing goes to the congregation before the family gives the wording.
2. **Draft the announcement in the pastoral voice, first person, from the clergy.** "It is with
   deep sadness that I share with you the death of our friend and member [Name], who died
   [when] [surrounded by family / peacefully / after a long illness — only what the family
   authorized]." Do not open with logistics.
3. **Name the tradition's practice.** For a Christian funeral: date and time of the visitation,
   funeral or memorial service, committal, and reception. For a Jewish funeral: the funeral (often
   within 24-72 hours), shiva schedule and location, and any restrictions (no flowers; make a
   donation in memory if the family requests). For a Muslim funeral: the janazah prayer time and
   location, burial arrangements, and how the community can support the family during the mourning
   period. Do not import another tradition's cadence.
4. **Include a specific way to help.** Meal train link, shiva minyan sign-up, hospitality for
   out-of-town family, transportation, childcare — whatever the family has authorized. "Let us
   know if you need anything" places the burden on the grieving family; a named coordinator with
   an email address does not.
5. **Close with a pastoral sentence, not an org sign-off.** "Please hold the [Family Name] family
   in your prayers this week" is pastoral; "For more information, contact the office" is not the
   close. Both can be present; the pastoral sentence must come last.
6. **Sign with the clergy's own name and role.** "Rabbi [Name]", "Pastor [Name]", "Imam [Name]",
   not "The Communications Team" or "[Org]".
7. **Channel discipline.** Congregational email is the default. Do not post the death to social
   media until the family has said publicly or given explicit permission — assume extended family
   and colleagues learn from social feeds. Never post a photograph of the deceased that the family
   has not selected.
8. **Follow-up cadence.** A week after the funeral, a short second note from clergy — inviting
   continued support of the family, naming any scheduled memorial (shloshim, forty-day memorial,
   month's mind, one-year yahrzeit or unveiling) — signals the community keeps mourning after the
   crowd goes home.

## Drafting a Crisis / Tragedy Response — Numbered Checklist

For local violence, mass-casualty events, disasters, or national moments where the community will
be looking for a word.

1. **Speed vs. accuracy.** Within hours for a local event that directly touched the community
   (the shooting was at our school, the fire was in our building); within a day for a regional
   event with likely direct connections (many members may have been at the concert, may know
   victims); within two to three days, or not at all, for distant national moments that don't
   uniquely require our voice. Do not confuse being first with being pastoral.
2. **Confirm facts you will state.** Number of dead, names, cause — only what has been publicly
   confirmed by authorities. Pastoral letters that get facts wrong lose the community's trust for
   years. "We do not yet know" is a legitimate pastoral sentence.
3. **Lament first, meaning second, action last.** Open by naming the pain honestly (this is what
   makes it pastoral). Only then move to any meaning-making the tradition offers, and only last to
   any action (prayer service, blood drive, donation link). Reversing this order — action first,
   lament as an afterthought — reads as institutional.
4. **Do not proof-text.** If you cite scripture or a sacred text, cite it (chapter and verse or
   the equivalent) so the reader can look it up, and choose a passage that actually speaks to this
   moment, not a favored verse repurposed. A misapplied text lands worse than no text.
5. **Name the tradition's practice without moralizing at outsiders.** "As we prepare our hearts
   for Yom Kippur this week, this news lands especially heavily" is invitation; "you should be
   preparing for Yom Kippur" is scolding. The message is legible to members and non-members alike.
6. **Prophetic without partisan.** Name the moral wrong (gun violence, hate, dehumanization)
   without naming a party, candidate, or election. If the moral wrong cannot be named without
   partisan reference, route through counsel and `nonprofit-faith-church-governance` first.
7. **Two to three short paragraphs.** Publish under the clergy's name. Consider whether a short
   in-person gathering (vigil, prayer service, open sanctuary hour) is the right pastoral response
   and name it in the letter.

## Liturgical / Annual Calendar Cadence

A faith-based org's annual pastoral cadence is pegged to its constituents' traditions, not to a
generic calendar. Describe the practice, do not endorse it, and never assume every reader observes.

- **Christian congregation cadence (Western)**: Advent letter (four Sundays before Christmas,
  usually the first Sunday of Advent), Christmas Eve/Day greeting, Ash Wednesday call to Lent,
  Holy Week note (usually Palm Sunday), Easter greeting, Pentecost, and a Christ the King or
  program-year-close note in late November. Ordinary-time weeks carry the weekly sermon-adjacent
  email but no seasonal letter. Eastern Orthodox congregations follow the Julian calendar for
  Pascha in many US jurisdictions — check the specific parish before pinning a date.
- **Jewish congregation cadence**: monthly Rosh Chodesh notes for those who mark them; a High
  Holy Days letter in Elul (roughly a month before Rosh Hashanah) covering shofar service,
  selichot, Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, and ticketing/security; Sukkot/Simchat Torah note;
  Hanukkah greeting; Purim/megillah reading; Passover (letter with seder resources, kashering
  information, sale of chametz); Yom HaShoah and Yom HaAtzmaut (with denominational variation);
  Shavuot; Tisha B'Av. Weekly Shabbat email is standard and typically goes out Thursday, not
  Friday, so members receive it before the start of Shabbat.
- **Muslim community cadence**: monthly community notes; a Ramadan letter shortly before the
  month begins with iftar schedule, taraweeh, community expectations around fasting, and a
  reminder for those who cannot fast; Eid al-Fitr greeting on the confirmed date; Hajj/Eid
  al-Adha letter; Muharram/Ashura note where the community observes; Mawlid where observed.
  Ramadan communications assume some members fast and some do not (pregnancy, illness, travel,
  age, menstruation) and never shame non-fasters.
- **Faith-based social service org cadence**: an interfaith org (federation, hospital, refugee
  agency, food bank with religious identity) pegs its annual pastoral cadence to the constituent
  communities it serves. A Jewish federation sends High Holy Days and Passover messages to donors
  and staff. A Catholic hospital marks Ash Wednesday, Holy Week, and Easter for staff and
  volunteers. A multi-faith social service org typically marks the three or four moments where a
  greeting from the ED lands (Ramadan/Eid, High Holy Days, Christmas/Easter, and one civic
  moment like Thanksgiving or MLK Day) rather than every observance of every tradition — trying
  to greet everything greets nothing well.

## Artifact-Specific Guidance

- **Illness / hospitalization notice**: only what the person or family authorized, in writing.
  Never publish a diagnosis, prognosis, or hospital location without explicit permission. "Please
  hold [Name] and her family in prayer this week; she has asked for privacy about details" is
  more pastoral than a specific medical bulletin.
- **Wedding / bar-bat mitzvah / baptism / aqiqah announcement**: framed in the tradition's
  vocabulary; consent from the family or couple before including names, dates, or photos; short.
- **Clergy misconduct disclosure to the congregation**: draft only in coordination with the
  org's counsel, the insurer (many policies have notification and communication requirements),
  denominational judicatory, and — separately — the media plan owned by `nonprofit-media-relations`.
  The pastoral letter to the congregation is not the press release. It names what happened (only
  what has been established or authorized to disclose), acknowledges harm, states what steps are
  underway (investigation, reporting to authorities, cooperation with the denomination), does not
  minimize, does not sensationalize, and does not name the accused if the investigation is not
  concluded and disclosure is not authorized. It gives a way for anyone with additional
  information to reach an independent investigator directly, not through the org. It closes with
  care for those most harmed and does not close by defending the institution. Written under
  senior clergy's name, not the communications team. Route reporting-obligation questions to
  `nonprofit-faith-employment-ministerial-exception`.
- **Denominational statement / pastoral letter**: when the bishop, presiding elder, general
  minister, or head of communion writes on a moral, theological, or policy matter after the
  denomination's governance process has adopted it, the pastoral letter carries the decision to
  the membership. Speak in the voice of the tradition ("Our tradition teaches…", "The Book of
  Order requires…", "The general convention has adopted…"), not in the voice of a partisan.
  Distinguish clearly among (a) matters the denomination teaches, (b) matters on which the
  denomination has taken a public position, and (c) matters left to individual conscience.
  Address vs. avoid vs. teach: address it when the tradition has spoken; avoid it when the
  tradition has not; teach on it when members are asking and the tradition has resources but no
  binding position.
- **Farewell letter (outgoing clergy)**: short, gracious, no unfinished business made public, no
  parting shots, no naming of individuals in disputes, gratitude specific enough to be real. The
  outgoing clergy names the ending, not the future direction of the community.
- **Arrival letter (incoming clergy)**: introduces self as pastor rather than as a resume, names
  the transition, names honor for the outgoing clergy, does not preview policy changes, invites
  first meetings. Both letters are coordinated with the search committee, denominational
  transition officer, and (where applicable) `nonprofit-faith-church-governance`.
- **Sermon-adjacent weekly congregation email**: extends the Sabbath/Sunday/Friday message into
  the week — a paragraph or two of pastoral framing plus that week's calendar. Not a full sermon
  recap; not a marketing newsletter; not a fundraising ask (those live in
  `nonprofit-faith-stewardship-giving`).
- **Prayer request / prayer-chain content**: only what the person or family has authorized for
  the chain, distinguished from what the clergy holds in pastoral confidence. Common discipline:
  the chain gets a first name and one sentence ("Sarah, for healing"), not a diagnosis; the
  clergy holds anything more privately. Restate the confidentiality rule at the top of every
  prayer-chain distribution so new members learn it.
- **Building milestone (dedication, cornerstone, mortgage-burning, deconsecration, closure)**:
  the pastoral letter marks meaning, not construction status. A deconsecration or closure letter
  is a pastoral act of grief and gratitude, not an operational communication — draft it with the
  same care as a funeral, because for many members it is one.

## Language and Register Discipline

- **Direct address that includes without excluding.** "Friends" or "beloved community" or
  "members and friends of [congregation]" reads to members without pushing out a first-time
  reader. "Fellow believers" or "brethren" is denomination-specific and may exclude even
  members who don't use that vocabulary — use only when it is genuinely the community's word.
- **Scripture / sacred text citation.** Cite chapter and verse or the equivalent locator so the
  reader can look it up (Isaiah 40:1; Sura al-Baqara 2:286; Pirkei Avot 2:16). Do not string
  three unrelated citations together; one, well-chosen, does more work than three. Do not
  proof-text — do not attach a verse to a pastoral point it does not actually make.
- **Naming the tradition's practice.** "As we approach Yom Kippur…" (invitation) rather than
  "You should be preparing for Yom Kippur…" (scolding). Assume some readers observe and some do
  not; write a sentence that lands for both.
- **Multi-faith staff and beneficiary contexts.** A Christian ED writing to an interfaith staff
  names her own tradition without imposing it ("This is Holy Week for those of us in the
  Christian tradition; I know some of you are observing Passover this week, and I wish you a
  meaningful chag"). A Jewish federation writing to a broader donor community names its Jewish
  identity clearly without assuming every reader is Jewish. A Muslim org's message to non-Muslim
  partners at Eid can explain the practice briefly without proselytizing.
- **Lament without sentimentality.** Pastoral communications are among the few professional
  communications where lament is not only permitted but expected. Name the pain in one honest
  sentence and stop — do not accessorize with adjectives ("this heartbreaking, devastating,
  crushing news"). One true sentence lands; three decorated ones read as performance.
- **Voice fidelity when a staffer drafts for clergy.** Interview the clergy for three or four
  actual phrases they would say aloud about the moment, and build the draft around those
  phrases. Do not paste in comms boilerplate ("Our thoughts and prayers are with…") — clergy
  do not talk that way, and members can hear the difference instantly. If the clergy has not
  finished processing the topic themselves (which is common on hard moments), draft short and
  honest, not long and polished.
- **Length and finish.** Most pastoral communications are shorter than their authors want. Two
  to three short paragraphs, signed by clergy, under the clergy's name — does more work than a
  full page. If the draft is over 300 words for a public-moment pastoral letter, ask what can go.

## Channels and Pacing

- **The pulpit is the primary channel** for the pastoral word on a moment the community is
  already gathering to process. A short plain email follows for those who weren't there.
- **Congregational email** carries most between-service pastoral communication. Send Thursday
  (not Friday) for Jewish communities so it lands before Shabbat. Send well before Maghrib during
  Ramadan so it lands before iftar. Send early morning rather than late evening for a hard piece
  of news, so readers have the day to absorb it, not the night.
- **Bulletin / order of service** carries dated logistical detail (funeral times, service
  schedules) that the pastoral email can then reference.
- **Website statement** is the right channel for a clergy misconduct disclosure or a formal
  denominational statement that will be linked to for months; keep it short, signed, and dated.
- **Social media** is usually the wrong channel for a pastoral moment; reposting the email or
  the website statement is fine, but writing pastoral content directly into a platform post
  strips the register. For a death, default to no social post at all unless the family requests
  it.
- **Silence is a channel too.** Sending nothing is a legitimate pastoral choice when the answer
  to the five-question test is "not our word to speak." Silence should be a decision, not a
  default.

## Confidentiality and Privilege

Pastoral communications live at the edge of several confidentiality regimes; get these wrong once
and it takes years to rebuild trust.

- **Clergy-penitent privilege** protects confidential communications made to clergy in the
  clergy's spiritual capacity — but the scope varies by state, and many states have narrowed or
  abolished privilege for disclosures of child abuse or neglect (mandatory-reporter statutes).
  Route any drafting that references or alludes to a confidential disclosure to
  `nonprofit-faith-employment-ministerial-exception` first for the reporting analysis.
- **Prayer-chain confidentiality**: publish only what the individual or family authorized for the
  chain, and remind the chain in writing at every distribution that the request is not to be
  forwarded outside the chain. Assume a chain leaks; write accordingly.
- **Hospital visitation privacy**: HIPAA does not directly bind clergy visits in most cases, but
  a hospital's clergy-visit list is HIPAA-governed and the hospital cannot share a patient's
  presence without patient consent. Do not name the hospital, the unit, or the diagnosis in any
  written communication without explicit family consent.
- **When a family gives permission to share**: get the permission in writing (a text or email is
  fine) and note the specific wording authorized. Do not extrapolate — permission to announce a
  death is not permission to disclose cause.

## Voice Fidelity When Drafting For Clergy

Most pastoral letters land or fail on whether they sound like the clergy actually talking. From
the drafter's seat:

1. **Interview the clergy for three or four phrases they would say aloud** about the moment,
   before drafting a single sentence. Ask what they are actually thinking, not what they think
   they should say.
2. **Build the draft around those phrases.** Do not paste in comms boilerplate, do not import
   language the clergy would never use in the pulpit, do not smooth away the clergy's honest
   register.
3. **Match the clergy's cadence.** Some clergy write long sentences with subordinate clauses;
   others write short declarative sentences. A staffer's draft that flattens either loses the
   voice.
4. **Leave the hard edges.** If the clergy is angry, uncertain, or grieving, the draft should
   carry that — appropriately controlled but not sanitized. A pastoral letter that has been
   comms-scrubbed reads as institutional even when the words are right.
5. **The clergy is the author of record.** Send the draft to the clergy with an explicit invite
   to change any sentence, and treat every edit they make as improving voice fidelity, not
   degrading polish. Publish under the clergy's own name.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Wrong register.** A brand-voice message ("At [Org] we believe…") sent when the community
  needed a pastoral word from clergy, or a pastoral message drafted when the community needed a
  clear executive statement about a decision. Fix by naming the register before drafting and
  matching it to the moment.
- **Saying something on everything.** The org posts a statement for every national tragedy, every
  civic moment, every denominational controversy — and the pastoral voice loses weight. Fix by
  running the five-question test honestly; the answer is often "not our word this time."
- **Silence when a word was expected.** The community's own children were at the school where the
  shooting happened, and the pulpit is silent Sunday. Fix by treating silence as a decision, and
  by defaulting to speaking when the community is directly touched.
- **Prophetic voice slipping into partisan endorsement.** Naming a moral wrong is prophetic;
  naming a candidate, a party, or an election crosses the Johnson Amendment and jeopardizes
  501(c)(3) status. Fix by routing named-issue drafts near elections to counsel and
  `nonprofit-faith-church-governance` before publishing.
- **Comms-scrubbed pastoral letters.** The draft is polished, on-brand, and sounds like nothing
  the clergy would ever say aloud. Fix by interviewing for actual phrases, publishing under the
  clergy's name, and treating clergy edits as voice fidelity rather than nits.
- **Proof-texting scripture over an unrelated pastoral point.** A verse is attached to a message
  it doesn't actually make; readers who know the text hear the mismatch. Fix by choosing one
  passage that genuinely speaks to the moment, citing chapter and verse, and stopping there.
- **Announcing what the family did not authorize.** The death, illness, or hospitalization notice
  discloses cause, location, or detail the family did not authorize. Fix by getting authorized
  wording in writing before publishing anything, and by defaulting to less rather than more.
- **Clergy-misconduct letter that reads as institutional defense.** The letter minimizes, waits
  for legal clarity before acknowledging harm, or defends the org before centering those most
  harmed. Fix by drafting under senior clergy's name (not comms), acknowledging harm before
  process, and giving those with information an independent channel — coordinated with counsel,
  insurer, denomination, and `nonprofit-media-relations`, but not written by them.
- **Wrong channel.** A pastoral death post on the church Instagram before the family has
  notified extended family; a full pastoral statement squeezed into a 280-character platform
  post. Fix by defaulting to pulpit-then-email for pastoral content, using the website for
  formal statements, and staying off social media unless the family or moment specifically
  calls for it.
- **Too long.** Five paragraphs where two would have worked; a page-long statement on a moment
  that needed a paragraph. Fix by asking what a member reading this on a phone at 6:45 a.m. needs
  and cutting the rest.
- **Denominational and congregational voices out of sync.** The bishop says one thing, the local
  clergy says another, members receive both the same weekend. Fix by aligning in advance
  through `nonprofit-faith-church-governance` when the moment is one the denomination will
  address.

## Practitioner vs. Advisor Framing

- **As the clergy person or faith-based ED**: your name is on the letter, and your voice is the
  point. Draft short, in your own words, and let staff polish rather than compose. Run the
  five-question test honestly before speaking on public moments — the pastoral voice loses power
  when it speaks on everything, and gains it when it speaks rarely and truly. For a death, get
  family consent in writing before any public word; for a crisis, lament before meaning-making
  before action; for a misconduct disclosure, sign it yourself and center those harmed. When you
  don't yet know what you think about a hard moment, the pastoral letter can be shorter and more
  honest for it — "I am still holding this" is a legitimate pastoral sentence. Route
  named-issue drafts near elections and any disclosure implicating clergy-penitent privilege out
  to counsel and to the relevant sibling skill before publishing.
- **As a staffer, chief of staff, denominational communications director, or outside advisor
  drafting for clergy**: your job is voice fidelity, not authorship. Interview the clergy for the
  three or four phrases they would actually say, build the draft around those phrases, and
  publish under their name. Resist the temptation to smooth every hard edge — the clergy's
  honest register is what the community will trust. Coordinate with, but do not defer to,
  marketing, legal, and media staff: they own their artifacts (brand copy through
  `nonprofit-brand-messaging`, executive messages through `nonprofit-executive-communications`,
  press pieces through `nonprofit-media-relations`, giving letters through
  `nonprofit-faith-stewardship-giving`), and you own the pastoral letter that must land in a
  different register than any of theirs. When a clergy misconduct crisis is unfolding, insist
  that the pastoral letter be drafted alongside — not by — the press response, so the
  congregation is not addressed in the register of a legal statement.
