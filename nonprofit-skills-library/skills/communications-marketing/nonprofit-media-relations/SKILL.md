# Nonprofit Media Relations

## When to Use This Skill

Use this skill for earned-media work: getting a nonprofit's news covered by journalists, and managing
public statements/spokesperson performance during a media moment. Typical triggers: a program launch,
a research/report release, a notable milestone (anniversary, major grant, disaster response), a
negative story breaking about the organization, or preparing an executive for a media interview.

**Boundary:** This skill covers press/journalist-facing communication specifically. Organic social
posting is `nonprofit-social-media`; the underlying voice/positioning this skill draws from is
`nonprofit-brand-messaging`; testimony before a legislative body or lobby-day meetings is
`nonprofit-legislative-advocacy`; mobilizing supporters via petitions/action alerts is
`nonprofit-grassroots-mobilization`. If the story's target is a journalist/editor, use this skill; if
the target is a legislator, use advocacy skills; if the target is your own supporter list, use
grassroots mobilization or the newsletter/social skills.

## Standard Deliverable: Press Release Structure

Use inverted-pyramid structure (most important information first, supporting detail after) — this is
the format editors expect and can cut from the bottom without losing the core story:

1. **Headline:** States the news, not the mission ("[Org] Opens Region's First Youth Mental Health
   Drop-In Center" not "[Org] Continues Its Commitment to Youth").
2. **Dateline + lead paragraph:** Who, what, when, where, why — answered in the first 2-3 sentences.
3. **Supporting paragraph(s):** Context, scale, significance; include one concrete data point.
4. **Quote from a named spokesperson** (usually the ED or board chair) that adds perspective, not a
   restatement of the lead — a quote that just repeats the headline is a wasted opportunity.
5. **Boilerplate** ("About [Org]") — pull verbatim from the messaging house
   (`nonprofit-brand-messaging`) so it's consistent across every release.
6. **Media contact block:** name, phone, email — a release without a live contact often gets ignored
   by reporters on deadline.
7. **###** or "-30-" to signal the end of the release, a wire-service convention some editors still
   expect.

## Journalist Targeting and Pitching

- Build a media list segmented by beat (local news, nonprofit/philanthropy trade press, issue-specific
  beat reporters covering the org's cause area) rather than blasting one generic release to every
  contact — beat-relevant reporters convert to coverage far more often.
- Pitch individually or in small tailored batches, referencing the reporter's past relevant coverage
  in the first line ("Saw your piece on [related topic] — thought this might interest you") — mass
  BCC pitches with no personalization have very low response rates.
- Lead the pitch email with the news hook in the subject line, not the org name; reporters triage
  inboxes on newsworthiness, not organizational familiarity.
- Time pitches around the news cycle: avoid Friday afternoons and major holidays; pitch morning-of
  for same-day local coverage, or several days ahead for feature-length pieces that need scheduling.
- Offer an exclusive to one outlet for a bigger story if trying to secure deeper coverage than a wire
  release would generate — most outlets are more likely to run a story if they have some ownership
  of it.
- Always have visual assets (photos, b-roll, infographics) ready to attach or link — broadcast and
  online outlets frequently pass on stories lacking usable visuals.

## Spokesperson Preparation (Standard Deliverable: Media Prep One-Pager)

Before any interview, prepare the spokesperson with:

1. **3 key messages** (pulled from the messaging house pillars) that must land regardless of
   questions asked — the "message discipline" principle: a trained spokesperson bridges back to these
   points rather than only answering literally.
2. **Bridging phrases** to redirect off-message or hostile questions back to key messages ("What's
   most important for people to understand is...", "The real story here is...").
3. **Anticipated hard questions** with drafted, vetted answers — especially for any known
   controversy, funding criticism, or recent negative coverage; never let a spokesperson walk in
   without having rehearsed the hardest likely question.
4. **"No comment" alternative:** never say "no comment" — it reads as guilt. Use "I don't have those
   details in front of me, but I can follow up" or redirect to what can be shared.
5. **Format-specific coaching:** print interviews allow more nuance; broadcast requires tight,
   quotable soundbites (aim for answers under ~20 seconds); live interviews need a calm, practiced
   opening line since there's no edit safety net.

## Crisis/Rapid-Response Statement Protocol

When a negative story breaks or a reporter calls about a sensitive issue:

1. Do not go silent and do not speculate — acknowledge receipt of the inquiry and commit to a
   response timeframe, even if it's "we'll have a statement within the hour."
2. Get the facts internally verified before any public statement; a factually wrong rapid response is
   worse than a slightly slower accurate one.
3. Draft a holding statement first if full facts aren't ready: acknowledges the situation, states
   values/commitment to addressing it, avoids admitting fault or assigning blame prematurely.
4. Route every crisis statement through the ED (and legal counsel if liability, safeguarding, or
   financial impropriety is implicated) before release — this is not a comms-team-alone decision.
5. Designate one single spokesperson for the duration of the crisis to avoid conflicting statements
   from multiple staff.
6. Coordinate the public statement with internal stakeholders (board, staff, affected clients) so no
   one learns the organization's public position from the news before hearing it internally.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Generic, mission-statement-heavy releases** with no actual news hook — editors pass immediately.
- **Mass, unpersonalized pitching** that damages the relationship with reporters for future pitches.
- **No visuals ready**, losing coverage opportunities to organizations that had usable photos/video.
- **Unprepared spokesperson** who answers literally instead of on-message, or who speculates on facts
  not yet confirmed during a live interview.
- **Saying "no comment"** or going silent during a negative story, which reads as evasive regardless
  of actual culpability.
- **Multiple staff freelancing statements** during a crisis, creating contradictions that become a
  second news story about organizational disarray.
- **Boilerplate drift** — different releases using different, uncoordinated "About us" language
  because they weren't pulled from the shared messaging house.

## Practitioner vs. Advisor Framing

- **As the practitioner** (comms staff): keep a live, beat-segmented media list and a pre-drafted
  crisis holding-statement template on hand before a crisis ever occurs — building these under time
  pressure during an actual incident is a common and avoidable failure.
- **As an advisor/consultant**: when brought in during or after a media crisis, prioritize getting a
  single spokesperson and a fact-verification process in place immediately — organizational chaos
  (multiple people talking to press) is usually the more urgent fix versus the specific wording of
  any one statement.
