# Nonprofit Succession Planning

## When to Use This Skill

Use this skill for planning the transition of executive leadership (ED/CEO, and by extension other single-point-of-failure senior roles) — both the emergency, unplanned-departure scenario and the strategic, planned-departure scenario. Concrete triggers:

- Building an **emergency succession plan** (what happens if the ED is suddenly unable to serve — illness, accident, abrupt resignation)
- Building a **strategic/long-range succession plan** for a known upcoming departure (retirement, planned exit in 1–3 years)
- Designing the ED search and transition timeline, including interim/acting ED arrangements
- Identifying, assessing, and developing internal successor candidates (leadership pipeline / bench strength)
- Advising a founder on founder-transition risk specifically (organizations where the ED is also the founder carry distinct risk)
- Structuring board roles during a transition (transition/search committee charter, board chair's role vs. HR committee)

Do **not** use this skill for: general staff morale, burnout, or turnover among non-executive staff (`nonprofit-staff-retention`); recruiting or onboarding new board members (`nonprofit-board-recruitment`); the broader change-communication and organizational-restructuring process around a transition, merger, or program pivot (`nonprofit-change-management`) — this skill owns the succession/leadership-pipeline mechanics; change-management owns the communication and org-design execution once a transition is underway.

## Core Framework: Two Plans, Not One

Nonprofit succession planning literature (see Annie E. Casey Foundation and BoardSource succession planning guidance) draws a sharp line between two distinct plans that are often conflated — conflating them is itself a common failure mode:

1. **Emergency succession plan (departure risk plan).** A short, standing document — every organization should have one regardless of the ED's current health or tenure — answering: who is the immediate acting authority if the ED cannot serve starting tomorrow (usually the deputy director, COO, or a named senior staff member, ratified in advance by board vote); what decisions that acting leader can and cannot make without board approval (e.g., signing authority thresholds, hiring/firing, contracts); who on the board is the point of contact; and where key passwords/accounts/signatory documents are stored. Review and re-ratify this annually regardless of other activity — a stale emergency plan (naming someone who has since left) is worse than none, because it creates false confidence.
2. **Strategic/long-range succession plan.** Activated 1–3 years before a known departure (planned retirement, term-limited tenure, or founder transition). This is a full project with its own timeline, not a document filed away: (a) board forms a transition/search committee distinct from the standing governance committee; (b) organizational readiness assessment — is the org financially stable enough, does it depend too heavily on the outgoing ED's personal relationships (a **founder's syndrome** risk flag: donor relationships, program credibility, or institutional memory concentrated in one person with nothing documented); (c) internal candidate development if a pipeline exists — assess readiness against a leadership competency framework, not just tenure; (d) decision on internal promotion vs. open search vs. interim-then-search; (e) search process and timeline (typically 4–9 months for an ED search); (f) transition period design — outgoing ED's role during the search (advisor vs. fully stepped back), interim leadership arrangement if a gap exists, and a defined handoff period (knowledge transfer, donor/funder introductions, staff introductions) capped at a specific length so it doesn't drift into shadow leadership that undermines the incoming ED's authority.

Founder transitions deserve their own risk flag: when the departing ED is also the founder, board and staff loyalty, donor relationships, and organizational identity are often entangled with that individual specifically. Name this explicitly in the readiness assessment and plan extra time and structure (e.g., a founder-emeritus or advisory role with clearly bounded authority, explicit donor re-introduction plan) rather than assuming a standard transition timeline applies.

## Instructions

### For hands-on practitioners (board chair, governance/HR committee, outgoing ED)

1. Confirm an emergency succession plan exists and is current; if not, draft one now using the elements above and get it ratified by full board vote — this should exist independent of any known departure.
2. If a departure is 1–3 years out (or being contemplated), form a small transition committee (typically 3–5 board members plus, in later stages, senior staff input) separate from routine governance business.
3. Run an organizational readiness assessment: financial position, dependency on the outgoing ED's personal donor/funder relationships, documentation of institutional knowledge (SOPs, funder history, program history), and staff leadership bench strength.
4. If internal candidates exist, assess them against a written leadership competency framework (strategic thinking, board relations, fundraising capacity, financial management, program oversight, people management) and build individual development plans (stretch assignments, mentoring by the outgoing ED, external training) well before the transition date — pipeline development takes 1–2 years minimum to be credible.
5. Decide and document: internal promotion, open external search, or interim-then-search. If interim leadership is needed, define the interim's scope and authority in writing and set an end date to avoid an open-ended "acting" role that creates ambiguity for staff and funders.
6. Build the search timeline (job description, search committee, sourcing, interviews, board vote) — budget realistically for 4–9 months for an ED search, longer for founder transitions.
7. Design the handoff period with explicit boundaries: what the outgoing ED will and will not be involved in post-departure (board seat, advisory role, no role), and a specific donor/funder re-introduction plan so relationships transfer deliberately rather than being assumed to transfer on their own.
8. For a founder transition specifically, name the founder's-syndrome risk to the board directly and build in extra runway and structure (bounded advisory role, explicit relationship-transfer plan) rather than treating it as a standard ED swap.

### For advisors/consultants guiding a nonprofit client

1. First ask whether an emergency succession plan exists at all — this is frequently absent even in well-run organizations and is a fast, low-cost first deliverable that de-risks the client regardless of any planned transition.
2. When engaged for a planned transition, insist on forming a distinct transition committee rather than routing the process through the full board or the standing governance committee, to keep the process focused and confidential where needed.
3. Conduct the organizational readiness assessment candidly, including naming founder's-syndrome or donor-concentration risk directly to the board even when uncomfortable — this is often the single highest-value insight an outside advisor provides, because internal stakeholders are frequently too close to the relationship to name the risk themselves.
4. Recommend realistic timelines to the board (search timelines routinely get compressed by anxious boards); a rushed ED search is one of the most common causes of a failed placement within 18 months.
5. Coordinate explicitly with `nonprofit-board-recruitment` if the transition also surfaces board composition gaps (e.g., the board lacks HR/search expertise), and with `nonprofit-change-management` for the internal/external communication plan once the transition is publicly underway — this skill's deliverable is the succession plan and search process design, not the communications rollout.

## Common Failure Modes

- No emergency succession plan at all, discovered only after a sudden departure when it's too late to plan calmly.
- Conflating the emergency plan with the strategic plan, producing one thin document that serves neither purpose well.
- Starting strategic succession planning too late (months, not years, before a known departure), leaving no time for internal candidate development.
- Ignoring founder's-syndrome risk — assuming a founder transition can run on the same timeline and structure as a standard ED swap.
- An open-ended "interim" arrangement with no defined end date or scope, creating prolonged ambiguity.
- No structured donor/funder relationship transfer plan, causing major gift and grant relationships to lapse with the departing ED.
- Letting the outgoing ED's post-departure role stay undefined, which risks shadow leadership that undermines the successor's authority.
